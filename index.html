Markets and investing, explained without assuming you already speak the language. Mostly about which claims survive a second look, and which quietly don't.

What's on the radar

  • Most published stock-market strategies stop working — and we know roughly why

    Researchers publish patterns that supposedly predict returns. McLean and Pontiff followed 97 of them afterwards. Returns fell 26% once tested on newer data but before publication — that portion was never real, just a pattern found by looking hard enough. They fell 58% after publication, so investors piling in erased another chunk. The clincher: the post-publication drop happens reliably in the US only, where big institutions move fastest. Practical rule: halve any advertised backtest before believing it.

  • Loan defaults at private lenders are rising faster than the headline number shows

    Defaults tracked across $189bn of private loans went from 1.84% to 2.73% in six months. But Moody's reckons about 65% of last year's defaults were quietly renegotiated rather than formally missed — which is how trouble gets resolved without ever being counted. In a market where lenders estimate their own loan values, the reported figure looks like a floor rather than a measurement.

  • What the three dissenting Fed officials actually argue

    If their disagreement is really about whether interest rates can fix supply-driven inflation at all, rather than about being tough, they should be arguing from worries about public expectations rather than from current price data. Their speeches would settle it, and nobody seems to have checked.

  • Why the profit in selling same-day options hasn't been competed away

    Either sellers are being paid for a rare disaster that genuinely can't be insured cheaply, or it just needs tools ordinary investors don't have. Funds packaging this trade are growing, which makes for a clean test: if the profit shrinks as they scale, it was the second.
