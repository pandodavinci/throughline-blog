What's on the radar

  • Nobody has added up how much of the AI profit boom is companies revaluing each other

    Four company-quarters are documented, and the mechanism is clear — see When an AI startup raises money, its investors book a profit below. What's missing is the total: across every large company holding a stake in a private AI lab, what share of reported profit growth comes from those marks rather than from selling things. No research house appears to have published that figure. Third-quarter results, out from mid-October, are the next chance to find out whether this is spreading or staying with a handful of names.

  • A theory paper argues tangled debt makes a system safer, not riskier

    Almost everything written about AI lending assumes the same thing: the more the banks, funds, chipmakers and AI labs owe each other, the more channels there are for trouble to spread. A model published this month gets the opposite result for lending between banks. When a struggling borrower can pledge what it is owed as security for new, higher-ranking debt, existing lenders are automatically diluted — and that turns out to move losses around efficiently enough that "indebtedness and connectedness are sources of stability, not fragility." Whether AI-related loans have anything like that structure is a genuinely open question, and answering it would need loan terms that aren't public. Worth holding as a caution against assuming the intuitive direction.

  • A widely followed bull case rests on a number nobody has separated out

    JPMorgan strategists raised their year-end S&P 500 target to 8,000 last week, on the argument that AI spending is finally being paid back through customer demand — pointing to cloud growth and order backlogs at Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft. Those are real operating measures, and a different thing from the paper gains on AI stakes described in When an AI startup raises money, its investors book a profit below. But the case cites the same three companies' "strong earnings" without saying how much of that is which — and two of them alone booked over $150bn of stake revaluations in the quarter being cited. The ambiguity that was an accounting curiosity a week ago now has a price target riding on it.

  • Why the profit in selling same-day options hasn't been competed away

    Either sellers are being paid for a rare disaster that genuinely can't be insured cheaply, or it just needs tools ordinary investors don't have. A second source now leans the same way as the first, and says so outright: the premium on individual stocks runs many times larger than on the index — Tesla's roughly 11× the S&P's — because company-specific risk is expensive to hedge. Suggestive, but still nobody has run the clean test: does the profit shrink as the funds packaging this trade grow?
