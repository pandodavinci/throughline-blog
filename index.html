What's on the radar

  • The Treasury's bond buying gets its first real test on 9 September

    The announcement moved long-term rates on 19 August; the purchases themselves start three weeks later — see Long-term borrowing costs fell on a Treasury plan that hasn't bought a bond yet below. From that date the question stops being rhetorical: do roughly $4 billion operations actually hold the thirty-year yield down, or do deficits, inflation and oil reassert themselves and the announcement effect fade completely? Before that, the new Fed chair gives his first Jackson Hole address on 28 August, into a symposium themed on financial innovation. Whether he says anything at all about the Treasury operating on the long end is the thing to listen for.

  • The market's alarm about AI financing doesn't seem to track any particular deal

    The cost of insuring Nvidia's debt jumped by a record amount in late July on fear of a rumoured $250bn guarantee. The deal that actually filed in August was $105bn — less than half — and the insurance cost widened further, past its July peak. On the day it filed, Nvidia's chief executive denied the circular-financing charge outright, and that didn't move it either. Whatever is being priced, it does not appear to be the terms of this transaction. Meanwhile a separate and much larger arrangement — reportedly up to $350bn to finance a customer's chip purchases, as opposed to backstopping its rent — was said to be in talks in July and has not turned up in any filing since. The worry is no longer only a market phenomenon, either: the Fed's July minutes, released 19 August, named "the financing of the rapid buildout of AI-related infrastructure" as a stability risk in its own right and said private credit "warranted close monitoring."

  • Nobody has added up how much of the AI profit boom is companies revaluing each other

    Four company-quarters are documented, and the mechanism is clear — see When an AI startup raises money, its investors book a profit below. What's missing is the total: across every large company holding a stake in a private AI lab, what share of reported profit growth comes from those marks rather than from selling things. No research house appears to have published that figure. Third-quarter results, out from mid-October, are the next chance to find out whether this is spreading or staying with a handful of names.

  • The private credit default rate may be a floor rather than a measurement

    Three separate ways a struggling loan avoids being counted. Around 65% of defaults get resolved as distressed restructurings, which never print as defaults. Boston Fed researchers find loans where the borrower pays interest by adding it to the balance instead of paying cash — which keeps the loan looking current — rose from 5.4% to 9.8% of the population between 2022 and 2026; one of the authors calls it "a sign of stress" plainly. And funds holding these loans have started refusing withdrawals, after redemption requests hit 13–14% of assets. Reported defaults are at a record 6% on one index. The interesting number is the one underneath it, and nobody has it. A caution on the published figures either way: two outlets recently gave flatly contradictory default rates for the same fund in the same quarter.

  • Why the profit in selling same-day options hasn't been competed away

    Either sellers are being paid for a rare disaster that genuinely can't be insured cheaply, or it just needs tools ordinary investors don't have. A second source now leans the same way as the first, and says so outright: the premium on individual stocks runs many times larger than on the index — Tesla's roughly 11× the S&P's — because company-specific risk is expensive to hedge. Suggestive, but still nobody has run the clean test: does the profit shrink as the funds packaging this trade grow?
