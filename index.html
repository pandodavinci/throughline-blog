What's on the radar

  • Nobody has added up how much of the AI profit boom is companies revaluing each other

    Four company-quarters are documented, and the mechanism is clear — see When an AI startup raises money, its investors book a profit below. What's missing is the total: across every large company holding a stake in a private AI lab, what share of reported profit growth comes from those marks rather than from selling things. No research house appears to have published that figure. Third-quarter results, out from mid-October, are the next chance to find out whether this is spreading or staying with a handful of names.

  • The market has started charging for circular AI financing

    Chip stocks shed over $1 trillion in a few weeks this month, and the financial press named the reason outright: chipmakers are also investors in, and guarantors for, their own customers' spending. The cost of insuring Nvidia's debt reportedly surged by a record amount. In the same fortnight, Nvidia's guarantee behind OpenAI's Ohio campus was cut from a reported $250bn to $105bn. Tempting to read as market pressure forcing a retreat — but the public timeline is too loose to tell that apart from two symptoms of the same scepticism. All of it is secondhand so far.

  • The private credit default rate may be a floor rather than a measurement

    Three separate ways a struggling loan avoids being counted. Around 65% of defaults get resolved as distressed restructurings, which never print as defaults. Boston Fed researchers find loans where the borrower pays interest by adding it to the balance instead of paying cash — which keeps the loan looking current — rose from 5.4% to 9.8% of the population between 2022 and 2026; one of the authors calls it "a sign of stress" plainly. And funds holding these loans have started refusing withdrawals, after redemption requests hit 13–14% of assets. Reported defaults are at a record 6% on one index. The interesting number is the one underneath it, and nobody has it.

  • A theory paper argues tangled debt makes a system safer — and AI lending doesn't look like the case it describes

    The paper's result: when a struggling borrower can pledge what it is owed as security for new, higher-ranking debt, existing lenders get automatically diluted, and losses move around efficiently enough that "indebtedness and connectedness are sources of stability, not fragility." Worth holding as a caution against assuming the intuitive direction. But the two AI-adjacent structures whose terms are actually public don't work that way. Hyperscaler bonds turn out to be missing even standard protections, and one large loan against chips repriced through a wider spread and tighter covenants between two facilities three months apart. Ordinary risk pricing, not automatic dilution. Two structures out of a mostly private universe — a lean, not an answer.

  • Why the profit in selling same-day options hasn't been competed away

    Either sellers are being paid for a rare disaster that genuinely can't be insured cheaply, or it just needs tools ordinary investors don't have. A second source now leans the same way as the first, and says so outright: the premium on individual stocks runs many times larger than on the index — Tesla's roughly 11× the S&P's — because company-specific risk is expensive to hedge. Suggestive, but still nobody has run the clean test: does the profit shrink as the funds packaging this trade grow?
