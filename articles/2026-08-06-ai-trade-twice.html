The AI trade sits in most portfolios twice

6 August 2026

Building AI costs an enormous amount of money — data centres, chips, power. Some of that gets paid for out of company profits, but a lot of it is borrowed. The interesting question is who is doing the lending, and the answer has changed fast.

Increasingly it is private credit: investment funds that lend money directly to companies, instead of the company borrowing from a bank or issuing bonds that trade publicly. These loans don't trade on any market, so there's no daily price for them.

Lending from these funds to AI-related companies has gone from roughly nothing to over $200 billion in a few years. Morgan Stanley expects a further $800 billion of data centre financing from private credit over the next two years alone.

The public side grew too. Total AI-related borrowing is tracking toward $570 billion. The largest cloud companies issued about $121 billion of bonds in 2025 — more than four times their own five-year average, and roughly 30% of all new corporate borrowing in the US.

The same bet, filed under two different labels

Here is why that matters for an ordinary portfolio.

Say you hold an S&P 500 index fund — a fund that simply buys all 500 companies in proportion to their size. Because it weights by size, and because a handful of AI-linked companies have become enormous, the ten largest holdings are now about 40% of it. You own a lot of AI whether or not you chose to.

Say you also hold some private credit, which many people have added in recent years because it pays a good yield and, on paper, its returns don't move in step with the stock market. That second part is the appeal: it looks like it zigs when stocks zag. But that sleeve is now substantially loans to the AI build-out.

So both holdings now depend on the same thing: whether all this AI spending ends up earning enough to justify itself. One is ownership of the companies doing the spending. The other is debt owed by the projects they're spending on. On a portfolio statement they sit in different rows and look like they balance each other.

They are not two different bets. They are one bet, written on two different pieces of paper.

Why the overlap is hard to see

The financing has moved deliberately toward private lenders and toward separate legal entities set up to hold a single project — which keeps the debt off the parent company's own accounts. Most of it is arranged by a short list of firms: Blackstone, Blue Owl, Apollo, Pimco, BlackRock.

A growing share then gets bundled up and sold on to other investors in slices, a process called securitisation. JPMorgan expects $30–40 billion of data centre deals packaged this way each year in 2026 and 2027 — up from about $27 billion in 2025, and by then 7–10% of that entire market.

Now add the standing complaint about private credit. Because these loans never trade, nobody gets a market price for them. The funds estimate what they're worth themselves. The Financial Stability Board, JPMorgan's Jamie Dimon, Bank of America and the US Department of Justice have each separately said they don't trust those estimates. Dimon's 2026 shareholder letter criticised the absence of "rigorous valuation marks" and warned losses would run higher than expected. The Justice Department has flagged "creative" valuations.

That criticism now attaches to the fastest-growing thing these funds own. If AI spending pays off later or smaller than hoped, the losses would show up in the corner of the market where they are hardest to see — while the stock-market side of the same bet is 40% of the index.

What this is, and what it isn't

This is not a prediction that anything breaks. The lending may be perfectly sensible and pay off on schedule. The profits underpinning the stock side are real rather than wishful — about 85% of S&P 500 companies beat expectations this quarter.

The narrower point is the durable one: the overlap doesn't show up in the way most people check their risk. It runs across two categories that are normally treated as separate, and one of them is valued by the people who own it.

The honest limit of this piece: properly measuring how closely these two move together would need daily prices for those private loans, and those prices don't exist. The evidence here is the flow of money — who is lending how much to whom — not a statistical estimate. No such estimate can currently be built, which is arguably the point.
