Throughline

Three Fed officials want rates to go up, not down

5 August 2026

The Federal Reserve sets the interest rate that everything else in the economy is priced off. On 29 July it left that rate where it was, at 3.50–3.75%, for the fifth meeting running. Holding steady isn't news. How the committee voted is.

The vote was 9–3. And all three of the officials who disagreed — Beth Hammack of Cleveland, Neel Kashkari of Minneapolis and Lorie Logan of Dallas — wanted rates to go up. Not down.

That's the part worth registering. For years the argument inside the Fed was about how fast to cut. Now there's a bloc arguing to tighten. If your mental picture of this committee formed during the cutting years, it's out of date in a specific way.

Five years is the number that matters

Inflation has now been above the Fed's 2% target for more than five years. That's long enough to stop being a temporary overshoot and start being just how things are. It also quietly ends the old "is this inflation temporary or permanent?" debate in a direction that catches out anyone still working from a 2024 picture.

The Fed's own statement blames part of it on supply shocks — meaning prices rising because things are genuinely harder to produce or ship, rather than because people are buying too much. It names energy specifically. Press coverage points to the war in Iran and to bottlenecks in AI-related capacity as the two forces splitting officials, though that framing comes from journalists rather than from the Fed itself.

The disagreement may not be about being tough on inflation

Here's why that supply-shock detail matters more than it looks.

Raising interest rates works by making borrowing expensive, which cools down how much people and businesses buy. It's a tool for too much demand. It does close to nothing about an oil shock or a shortage of power capacity — you can't raise rates into producing more electricity.

So a committee facing supply-driven inflation is in a genuine bind. Raising rates imposes real, measurable pain on the demand side of the economy in exchange for limited effect on what's actually pushing prices up.

Which suggests the 9–3 split might not be a disagreement about how tough to be. It might be a disagreement about whether this tool works on this problem at all. If that's right, the three dissenters should be arguing from a specific worry — that people are starting to simply expect high inflation and act accordingly, which becomes self-fulfilling and is something rates can address. Reading their speeches would settle it.

Something else changed quietly

The Fed's rate is 3.50–3.75%. The interest rate on a 10-year US government bond is about 4.70%. Longer-term borrowing costs more than short-term borrowing.

That sounds unremarkable, and normally it is — but for years it was the other way round, which is unusual and historically a warning sign. It has now quietly returned to normal, and that changes the maths on whether to hold cash or longer-dated bonds. There's no announcement when this happens; it just happens.

The 10-year rate is easing back from an 18-month high. Market commentary makes the case for it climbing past 5%, which is the weakest link in the current consensus — it rests on a story rather than a measurement.

Erratum · 6 August 2026

The paragraph above repeated a widely-quoted market view: that the term premium is unusually low but rising, and that its return to normal would push the 10-year rate past 5%.

Term premium is the extra yield investors demand for lending for ten years rather than rolling over short-term loans — compensation for being locked in while things could change. The New York Fed publishes an estimate of it. That estimate contradicts the claim.

DateEstimated term premium, 10-year
30 April 20260.73%
May 20260.667%
June 20260.513%

It is falling, not rising. And that changes the interpretation rather than just fixing a number.

A 10-year rate can be split into two parts: what the market expects short-term rates to average over the decade, plus the term premium on top. In April, a 4.45% yield was 3.72% expectations plus 0.73% premium. Since then the 10-year rate has risen to about 4.70% while the premium fell — so the increase has to be coming from the expectations part. Investors are pricing in higher Fed rates ahead.

Which means the bond market is effectively siding with the three dissenters. It also means the case for a 5% ten-year should rest on where Fed policy goes, not on the term premium normalising. Those two stories call for different protection, and only one of them matches the data.
