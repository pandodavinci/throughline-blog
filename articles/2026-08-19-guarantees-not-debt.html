The biggest commitments in the AI build-out are guarantees, which don't count as debt

19 August 2026

The money behind AI data centres is usually discussed as borrowing — who lent it, how much, at what rate. But several of the largest individual commitments disclosed this year are not loans, and the companies making them have not borrowed anything. They have written guarantees: promises to cover someone else's obligation if it goes wrong.

That distinction is not a technicality. A loan appears on a balance sheet as a debt from the day it is drawn. A guarantee, if the company judges that paying out on it is unlikely, appears as no liability at all — only as a note in the accounts. The commitment is real, the disclosure is genuine, and the number is nowhere in any figure a reader would think to check.

The clearest example is in Meta's own filing

Meta holds a 20% stake in a joint venture building a data centre in Louisiana, entered into in October 2025. In its quarterly report to the US Securities and Exchange Commission for the period ending 30 June 2026, the company sets out what it has promised.

The instrument is a residual value guarantee. Meta will lease the facility; if it walks away at the end of the lease and the building turns out to be worth less than an agreed figure, Meta covers the shortfall. The agreed figure — the threshold — is approximately $28 billion, declining over time.

Add the guarantee to Meta's investment in the venture, its lease commitments and its expected future funding, and the filing states the company's total maximum exposure to loss is $46.03 billion. Against that, one sentence: residual value guarantee payments "are not probable, and therefore no liability has been recorded to date."

Nothing there is hidden, and "not probable" is the standard accounting test rather than an opinion Meta invented. The point is narrower and stranger: a disclosed maximum exposure and a recorded liability are different things, and only the second one reaches the debt and leverage figures that analysts, index providers and ordinary investors actually read.

The timing is worth sitting with too. The leases this guarantee attaches to do not begin until 2029, with an initial four-year term and renewal options running out to twenty years. The commitment was made in 2025 for a building that will not be occupied for another three years, and the question of whether it is worth what everyone assumed will not be asked until the 2030s.

Nvidia's version is larger and moves faster

On 17 August 2026, a securities filing disclosed that Nvidia will provide up to $105 billion, structured as a guarantee, backing OpenAI's twenty-year lease of a new campus in Pike County, Ohio. Nvidia is also putting $1.5 billion directly into the developer, SoftBank-backed SB Energy, and is the site's exclusive chip supplier. First phase online in 2028.

So the chip company is simultaneously selling to the customer, investing in the customer's landlord, and standing behind the customer's rent. If OpenAI cannot pay, Nvidia's guarantee is what makes the project financeable — which means the demand Nvidia reports is partly demand Nvidia has underwritten.

The size of that promise has also proved unusually mobile. Reporting in late July and early August had Nvidia negotiating to backstop up to $250 billion for the wider build-out; by the time the filing landed, the Ohio figure was $105 billion. A commitment of that scale being more than halved inside about a fortnight is not how borrowing behaves. It is how a negotiated promise behaves.

Somebody is now suing over exactly this

The question of when a company has to tell bondholders about financing plans of this kind is being tested in court. In Ohio Carpenters' Pension Plan v. Oracle, filed in New York in January 2026, investors who bought $18 billion of Oracle bonds allege the offering documents described further AI-related borrowing as merely "potential" when specific plans already existed. Seven weeks after the bond sale, Oracle sought a further $38 billion tied to the same OpenAI agreement. The defendants named include Oracle, Larry Ellison, former chief executive Safra Catz, and sixteen underwriting banks.

These are allegations, and the case has not been tried. What makes it worth watching is that a ruling either way would function as a disclosure standard for the whole structure — reached through litigation rather than through any regulator writing a rule. The Financial Stability Board and the Bank for International Settlements have both flagged the visibility problem in AI financing and neither has acted on it.

One detail from the same case cuts against reading markets as asleep: the cost of insuring Oracle's debt against default reached its highest level since 2009 in December 2025, before the lawsuit was filed. Somebody was already repricing the risk.

What this adds up to

A law firm tracking disputes in this area estimates that roughly $120 billion of data-centre spending moved off balance sheet within an eighteen-month window. That is the shape of the thing: not more leverage than anyone realises, necessarily, but leverage held in a form that the usual measurements do not capture.

And there is a reason guarantees keep showing up rather than ordinary loans. A guarantee is what you write when the party doing the spending cannot borrow the money on its own credit, and someone further up the chain wants the spending to happen anyway. It is the natural instrument for a supplier financing its own customer.

The largest promises in the build-out are being made by the companies that benefit most if the build-out continues.

In August 2026 the market started charging for this. Chip stocks shed more than $1 trillion in value in a matter of weeks, with financial press naming "circular financing" as the reason outright, and the cost of insuring Nvidia's own debt reportedly surged by a record amount. Whether that pressure is what shrank the Ohio guarantee from $250 billion to $105 billion, or whether both are just symptoms of the same scepticism about AI spending, cannot be told apart from the public timeline.

The narrower claim survives regardless. Read a hyperscaler's balance sheet today and you will not find these commitments in the debt figures, because they are not debt. You will find them in the notes, correctly disclosed, in numbers large enough to matter — and nobody has added them up across companies. That total does not exist anywhere.

What this piece does not establish. Meta's disclosure is complete and its accounting treatment is standard; nothing here suggests concealment, and the $46.03bn figure is quoted from Meta's own filing precisely because the company published it. The Nvidia guarantee, the Oracle complaint's details and the $120bn off-balance-sheet estimate are all secondhand — relayed through press reporting and a law firm's own client alert, not read in the underlying filings or pleadings. The Oracle allegations are untested and the case is pre-trial. Note also how easily these numbers get garbled: at least one outlet covering the Meta guarantee reported the $27bn total development cost of the venture as though it were the $28bn guarantee threshold, which are separate figures in the filing. And "maximum exposure to loss" is a worst case by construction — it is not a forecast, an expected loss, or a claim that any of this will be paid.

Erratum · 21 August 2026

Two passages above treat it as established that Nvidia's Ohio guarantee shrank — "the size of that promise has also proved unusually mobile… a commitment of that scale being more than halved inside about a fortnight," and later the question of "whether that pressure is what shrank the Ohio guarantee from $250 billion to $105 billion." A closer look at the dates does not support the premise those sentences rest on. There is no evidence a $250 billion commitment ever existed to be halved.

The sequence, checked against the filing rather than against press summaries:

DateWhat happened
27–28 July 2026Nvidia shares fell about 5%; the cost of insuring its debt widened by the most in a single day since that contract began trading. Reporting attributed it to fear of a potential $250bn guarantee for a not-yet-announced deal.
17 August 2026The deal is announced and filed at $105bn. Nvidia's filing makes no reference to any earlier figure and is not written as a revision of one.
18 August 2026The cost of insuring Nvidia's debt widens again, past its late-July peak.

So the $250 billion was a rumoured upper bound that the market priced before any deal existed — not a commitment Nvidia walked back. What the record actually shows is a market that feared a number, got one less than half as large, and grew more worried anyway. That reading undercuts the "market pressure forced a retreat" story the original paragraph gestured at, in the opposite direction from a simple factual fix: the concern does not appear to be anchored to this deal's terms at all.

One detail sharpens the point. On the day the deal filed, Nvidia's chief executive publicly rejected the circular-financing characterisation outright — "No. OpenAI will pay the lease" — and the insurance cost on Nvidia's debt kept climbing regardless. A direct denial from management that fails to move the pricing is stronger evidence than silence would have been that the market is not reacting to this transaction's facts.

Separately, the filing is more precise than the article's "up to $105 billion" suggests. Nvidia's obligation is "cumulatively capped at $105 billion for its initial commitment," covering roughly 4.25 gigawatts of capacity, with a further 3.8 gigawatts available at Nvidia's own discretion. For this deal as filed, $105 billion is a floor on the eventual commitment rather than a ceiling on Nvidia's possible exposure at the site.
