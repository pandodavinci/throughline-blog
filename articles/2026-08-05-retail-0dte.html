Small investors crowded into the one options trade that reliably loses

5 August 2026

An option is a contract that pays off if a market moves a certain way by a certain date. You pay a small amount up front for the chance at a larger gain. If the move doesn't happen by the deadline, the contract expires worthless and you lose what you paid.

The deadline can be months away, or it can be today. Contracts expiring the same day they're bought are called 0DTE — zero days to expiry. They're cheap, because there's very little time left for anything to happen, and they're now overwhelmingly what small investors trade. More than 75% of their trades in S&P 500 options are these same-day contracts.

They are also, on the evidence, the worst-performing thing available to them.

Two separate research teams measured this independently and landed in the same place — one at 4.7% worse, the other at around 3%. Two groups agreeing is what makes this worth believing, more than either number alone.

The problem is the fee, not the forecast

This is the part worth getting right, because it changes what you'd do about it.

Every trade has a hidden cost called the spread: the gap between the price you can buy at and the price you can sell at. It goes to the dealer in the middle. On a $100 contract, a $1 spread is 1%. On a $2 same-day contract, that same $1 gap is 50%.

Same-day options are cheap, so the spread eats a huge share of what you paid. The losses are mostly this, not bad predictions.

Which means the intuitive fix doesn't work. Better timing, sharper signals, more discipline about which direction you bet — none of it touches the leak. Small investors actually get slightly better prices than the headline quotes suggest, and they still lose. The improvement isn't remotely enough.

There is real money here. It goes to the other side.

Now the genuinely interesting bit.

You can be on either side of an option: the buyer paying for the chance of a payoff, or the seller collecting that payment and taking the risk. Between September 2016 and January 2026, these same-day contracts were consistently priced for more movement than actually occurred. In plain terms: buyers systematically overpaid for the possibility of a big move that usually didn't come.

That gap is real and statistically solid. It goes to the sellers.

So the picture is a genuine, persistent source of profit sitting inside the exact contract where the biggest group of participants reliably loses money. That's only a paradox until you notice they're the ones on the losing side of it.

Whether it lasts is the open question. If sellers are being paid for a rare disaster — most days you collect, occasionally you get badly hurt — then it's fair compensation and it persists. If it only exists because selling at scale needs tools and balance sheet that small investors don't have, it should shrink once funds package it up and sell it to everyone. Funds that do exactly this are growing, which makes for a natural test. Given how much apparent profit in this field vanishes under scrutiny, the first explanation is the safer bet.

One thing is genuinely unresolved. Older research found small investors buying these contracts; newer work says they sell them, but only at certain times of day. That matters — buying is lottery-ticket behaviour, selling is collecting insurance premiums, and the two say very different things about who's really on the other side.
