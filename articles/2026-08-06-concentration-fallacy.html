Selling stocks because the index looks top-heavy cost 0.9% a year for a century

6 August 2026

An S&P 500 index fund buys all 500 companies in proportion to their size. Bigger company, bigger slice. That means when a few companies get very large, the fund quietly becomes much less spread out than the name "500 companies" suggests.

That is where we are. The ten biggest names are now about 40% of the index — roughly double their share a decade ago. The largest seven alone were 33.8% in June 2026. Concentration passed its previous record, set in 1932.

The factual part of the worry is just true: roughly forty cents of every dollar you put in goes to ten companies. Whatever you thought you were buying, that is a concentrated position.

The useful question is different. Should you do anything about it? A recent study answers that directly, and the answer is no.

How they tested it

Mark Kritzman and David Turkington, writing in the Financial Analysts Journal, ran two approaches over 1926 to 2025 — a full century.

  • The cautious approach: move money out of stocks whenever the index gets more top-heavy, back in when it spreads out again.
  • The do-nothing approach: hold a fixed amount in stocks and ignore concentration entirely.

The clever part is that they forced both approaches to hold the same average amount in stocks over the century — 67.8%. That matters. Without it, the cautious approach would automatically look worse simply because it held fewer stocks, and stocks generally go up. Equalising the average means you're testing the signal — does reacting to concentration help? — rather than just rediscovering that owning stocks pays.

Reacting to concentration didn't just fail to help. It earned less and was more volatile, and ended the century with less than half the money. Used the obvious way, the warning signal actively hurt.

What still stands

Two different claims usually get bundled together in this argument. The study separates them, and only one of them dies.

  • "Sell when the index gets top-heavy." Dead. It has a hundred-year record and the record is bad.
  • "A top-heavy index means lower returns over the next decade." Untouched. Goldman Sachs's David Kostin argues exactly this. A signal can be useless for deciding when to act while the overall level still tells you something about the next ten years — so this study doesn't refute him.

Treat the scarier numbers with care even so. Tema ETFs put today's concentration as implying about −5% a year over the next decade. That figure comes from extending a historical pattern forward, and the pattern rests on a handful of past episodes that aren't really independent of each other — the 1930s and the 1960s aren't two clean separate experiments. It's an extrapolation, not a measurement.

For perspective: today's levels are high but not unprecedented. The market was at least as concentrated in the 1930s, 1950s and 1960s.

What the study doesn't cover is worth knowing. It tests exactly one response — reduce your stock holdings when concentration rises. It doesn't test buying an equal amount of each company instead of weighting by size, capping how much any single company can be, or spreading into other countries. Those all respond to the top-heaviness without trying to time the market. The study narrows the question rather than closing it.
