The lenders financing AI also lent heavily to what AI might replace

17 August 2026

A single corner of the lending market has managed to position itself on both sides of the same question. It has lent hundreds of billions of dollars to build AI, and hundreds of billions more to the software companies that AI is most often said to threaten. Those two exposures are discussed everywhere as separate stories. They are downstream of one variable.

The corner in question is private credit: investment funds that lend directly to companies, rather than the company borrowing from a bank or issuing bonds that trade publicly. These loans have no market price, because they never change hands — a detail that matters later.

The first exposure: paying for the build-out

Data centres, chips and power cost an enormous amount, and a growing share of that bill is borrowed rather than paid out of profits. Private credit has become a principal lender to it. Loans from these funds to AI-related companies now stand at over $200 billion, according to Bank for International Settlements estimates, projected to reach $300–600 billion by 2030.

That much has been widely covered. It is the visible half.

The second exposure: lending to the incumbents

The other half is a book these same funds have been building for a decade, for reasons that had nothing to do with AI.

Subscription software companies — the sort that sell a business tool for a monthly fee, sometimes called SaaS — turned out to be excellent borrowers. Their revenue is contractual and repeats. Lenders liked that, and lent accordingly. Outstanding private loans to these companies grew from roughly $8 billion in 2015 to over $500 billion by the end of 2025. They are now 19% of all direct private lending, and about one private credit fund in three holds some.

That was a sound thesis for ten years. It is now a bet that AI does not do to those companies what a great many people are paid to argue it will.

The market has already run a rehearsal

This is the part that lifts the story above speculation, and it happened before most people were framing it as a risk at all.

Between October 2025 and February 2026, shares in software companies fell about 30% on fears that AI would erode their business. The interesting question is whether anyone repriced the debt of those companies at the same time — and normally you couldn't tell, because private loans have no price.

There is one place to look. A business development company, or BDC, is a fund that holds private loans but is itself listed on a stock exchange. Its own shares trade every day. That gives you something otherwise unavailable: a live public price for a bundle of loans that have none.

Over that same window, BDCs fell about 10%. And BDCs with heavy software exposure underperformed the rest by roughly five percentage points. Their share prices also slipped further below the value the funds themselves said their loan books were worth — investors, in other words, declining to take the stated valuations at face value.

That is a measured market reaction rather than a forecast. When the equity market got frightened about AI disrupting software, the lenders to software were marked down in proportion to how much of it they held. The mechanism was tested, at small scale, and it worked as described.

What the scenarios say

Two banks have since put numbers on how bad the second exposure could get. Both estimates are secondhand — relayed through press coverage rather than read in the original notes — and should be treated as indicative.

UBS strategists led by Sachin Ganesh estimate that around 35% of the $1.7 trillion private credit market faces some AI-disruption risk, concentrated in technology and business services, and that in an aggressive disruption scenario default rates could reach 13%. Morgan Stanley separately forecasts around an 8% default rate in direct lending from late 2026 through 2027, led by software, against a historical average of 2–2.5%. Morgan Stanley characterises its own figure as "significant but not systemic," noting that borrowing across the industry is less leveraged than in 2008.

A default rate is simply the share of loans where the borrower stops paying or has to renegotiate terms. The two estimates measure different things — UBS is sizing a whole market's risk, the BIS figure above is a bottom-up count of an actual loan book — so they are not rival estimates of one number. What they share is a conclusion that the exposure is a double-digit percentage of the asset class rather than a rounding error.

For context, private credit defaults are already at their highest level in at least five years without any of this. One industry index covering 697 loans recorded a 2.73% default rate in the first quarter of 2026, up from 1.84% two quarters earlier.

What it means

Here is the connection nobody in the source material makes.

If AI capital spending disappoints — if the revenue arrives later or smaller than the build-out assumed — then the loans financing that build-out come under strain, for the obvious reason. But the software loans do not get a reprieve in that scenario. A company can be damaged by an AI competitor without that competitor ever earning enough to justify what was spent building it. Disruption and profitability are separate outcomes, and the unhappy combination is the one where both legs sour together: the build-out loans because the money never came, the software loans because their borrowers were undermined anyway.

Two exposures that look like independent sector bets, running off the same uncertainty in opposite directions.

The reason this is worth stating is that every source covering it treats the two as unrelated — including pieces that discuss both in the same article. Data-centre financing is filed as an AI story; software lending is filed as a credit-quality story. If any fund is actually tracking them as connected exposures, it has not said so publicly.

And the place where all this sits is the one that shows stress last. These loans are valued by the funds that own them, which is the standing complaint about the asset class from the Financial Stability Board, from Jamie Dimon, and from the US Department of Justice. The BDC discounts described above are one of the few outside opinions available on whether those valuations are right.

What this piece does not establish: that the two exposures actually move together. Nobody has measured that, and measuring it would need prices for loans that don't have any. The BDC evidence tests one leg — that software lending gets marked down when AI frightens the software market — not the correlation between the legs, which is the claim that would matter most. The UBS and Morgan Stanley figures are relayed through press reports rather than read at source. And a scenario forecast is not a base case: both banks are describing what an aggressive disruption would do, not predicting one. There is also a live argument, from a recent theoretical paper on interbank lending, that dense networks of debt can be stabilising rather than fragile — whether anything like that applies here is unknown.
