When an AI startup raises money, its investors book a profit

16 August 2026

Some of the largest profits reported by American companies this year were not earned by selling anything. They were earned by owning a piece of an AI startup that raised money at a higher price.

This is not an accounting scandal. It is what the rules require. But it means a meaningful slice of what gets described as an "AI earnings boom" is something closer to a valuation loop, and it is worth being able to tell the two apart.

What happened

Company profits are usually reported per share — total profit divided by the number of shares, called earnings per share, or EPS. It's the number analysts forecast and the number a company either beats or misses. Anything that moves profit moves EPS.

In the second quarter of 2026, S&P 500 companies were on track for roughly 50% profit growth against the same quarter a year earlier, with 86% of companies beating forecasts — the highest beat rate since 2021. Extraordinary, on the face of it.

It was also extremely concentrated. Remove just two companies — Alphabet and Amazon — and the degree to which the index as a whole beat expectations falls from 29.2% to 10.9%. Two names out of five hundred accounted for roughly two-thirds of the surprise.

What those two companies beat on is the interesting part. Alphabet's quarter included $98bn of gains on equity stakes it holds — gains on paper, nothing sold. Amazon's included $53.4bn from its investment in Anthropic, the AI lab.

It had happened the quarter before, too. In the first quarter of 2026, Amazon booked $16.8bn of gains on its Anthropic stake — more than half of its $30.3bn total profit — of which $12.3bn came from a single revaluation triggered when Anthropic raised a new funding round. Alphabet's $62.6bn quarterly profit included about $28.7bn from the same source, also close to half.

And it is not confined to those two. Microsoft's own earnings release for the quarter ending December 2025 states that net gains on its OpenAI investment added $7.6bn to net income and $1.02 to EPS. Microsoft stripped that gain out of its adjusted figures specifically so investors could see how the business performed without it — a fair signal that the company itself treats this as something other than core earnings.

Why it happens, and why it keeps happening

When you own shares in a listed company, working out what your stake is worth is easy: look up the price. A stake in a private company like Anthropic or OpenAI has no price, because the shares don't trade.

A rule introduced by the US accounting standard-setter in 2018 says what to do about that. When a private company you hold a stake in raises money — a new funding round, a follow-on investment, a share sale between investors — that transaction sets an observable price. At that point the investor must revalue its own holding to match, and the change in value flows straight through to reported profit.

So the trigger isn't the investor's performance, and it isn't even the startup's performance. It's the startup's fundraising calendar. Anthropic and OpenAI have been raising roughly every couple of quarters at rising valuations. Their backers — Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Nvidia — are large enough that a single revaluation moves group-level EPS. That combination produces this pattern most quarters, from whichever investor happened to have a holding reprice.

Robert Willens, an accounting consultant, put the awkward version of it plainly: "the more they invest in Anthropic, the more profit they can report — without Anthropic ever having to pay them a dollar." His sharper point is that an investor can influence the marked value of its own asset through its own follow-on investment decisions — put more money in at a higher price, and the stake you already held is revalued upward too.

The part that argues against reading too much into it

There's a good reason to think this is honest measurement rather than a gimmick: it goes down as well as up. In the same release showing a $7.6bn gain, Microsoft disclosed that the equivalent quarter a year earlier had recorded a $939m loss on that same OpenAI stake.

That matters more than another quarter of gains would. A mechanism that only ever produces good news deserves suspicion. One that produced a loss when the mark moved the other way is doing what fair-value accounting is supposed to do.

It's also worth being clear that the rest of the earnings season was real. Even after removing Alphabet and Amazon, the index still beat expectations by more than its five- and ten-year averages, and Energy, Communication Services, Consumer Discretionary, Information Technology and Materials all posted double-digit growth. This is not a story about fake earnings. It's a story about which earnings tell you what.

What it means

The bull case for US equities over the past two years has leaned on a specific claim: that share prices are rising because profits are rising, rather than because investors are simply willing to pay more for each dollar of profit. The first is considered sturdier ground than the second.

This mechanism blurs that distinction. When AI valuations rise, the reported profits of the companies holding AI stakes rise with them. Rising valuations turning into rising earnings, which are then cited as justification for rising valuations, is a loop — and part of what looks like earnings growth is enthusiasm wearing an earnings-growth costume.

How much? Genuinely unknown, and that's the honest answer. What can be said is narrower and still useful: when a company holding large stakes in private AI labs reports a big profit beat, the first question worth asking is how much of it came from marking those stakes, and the answer is usually in the earnings release.

Three limits on this. First, nobody has published the index-wide figure — what fraction of all large-cap profit growth comes from this channel, rather than the four company-quarters described here. The next real checkpoint is third-quarter results, reported from mid-October. Second, Microsoft's release doesn't specify which accounting treatment applies to its OpenAI stake, so while the shape matches Alphabet's and Amazon's, it isn't confirmed to run through the identical rule. Third, this piece describes a distortion in how profits are reported. It is not a claim about whether the underlying AI investments will prove to be good ones.
